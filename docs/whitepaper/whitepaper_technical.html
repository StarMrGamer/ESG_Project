ASEAN ESG Momentum Radar

An investment-research tool that interrogates before it answers, scores ESG evidence deterministically, bridges that read to the company's own financials, and then takes a position against the published rating — disagreeing with dated evidence rather than picking a stock.

1 · The problem

A rating is a level, published late and updated slowly. The investor gets a snapshot with no way to tell whether it is still true today, which piece of evidence holds it up, or what would change it. A rating cannot show its working; it can only be believed.

Staleness is measurable, not theoretical. Two constituents of the current ASEAN ESG-improver basket we work from — Malaysia Airports (Feb 2025) and Intouch Holdings (Apr 2025) — went private while still listed as members. The list did not move.

The ESG read and the money read are never bridged. "Improving on ESG" and "making money" are answered in different tools, so the question an investor actually holds — is this worth my attention, and what would change that — is answered by nobody. A company can improve on ESG and still be a bad business.

And screening tools hand you a dashboard. A wall of tiles pushes the interrogation back onto the user, who must already know which axis matters before the tool is any use.

Problem areas addressed: ESG/sustainable-investing analytics; evidence provenance and verification; investor decision support for ASEAN equities.

2 · The solution — six steps, in order

1 · AskFour short questions set the mandate, the risk tier, the evidence horizon and the target — locally, with no model call.
2 · GatherFour disjoint search angles per company — emissions, governance, financing, controversy — return dated, sourced events. Controversy is deliberate: a sweep must not quietly become a press-release collector.
3 · ScoreThe model reads; rules score. The LLM never sets a direction, a weight or a label. Scoring is a pure function: same inputs → same run id, same numbers, every time.
4 · BridgeThe ESG verdict is joined to a financial read from the company's own verified figures, and reported beside the score — never folded into it.
5 · ChallengeEvery verdict ships with the case against it: cons on both axes, plus which single piece of evidence, if removed, would change the answer.
6 · RecordOne hash per run, written once to a public ledger, so a verdict given in 2026 can be checked in 2028 without trusting us.

4 · The differentiator — it takes a position

Disagreement is signed and means one specific thing: our evidence-based momentum percentile minus the incumbent rating's percentile — which is exactly why the output can be argued with. It places every company in one of four quadrants (hidden winner, consensus, overrated leader, value trap), so a deteriorating, highly rated name can never be flattered by a high score the way a blended composite would allow.

What it will never do. No buy, sell or hold; no published score, no ranking, no pick. Those are regulated claims, and they are weaker than what we can prove: a disagreement with a named rating, backed by dated sources, that moves when the evidence moves.

5 · Bridging ESG and the financial read — and why it is a gate, not a term

Folding an earnings term into the disagreement score would end the argument: nobody, including us, could then say what +0.6 claims. It would stop being a disagreement with a rating and become a composite pick. So the financial read is computed after the scoring and reported beside it — a context gate. The separation is enforced by test: the scoring engine cannot import the financial module.

The financial verdict is taken from two fiscal years of verified figures the basket already carries, with no external fetch. Across the 52: 17 strong · 22 adequate · 11 weak · 2 unknown. unknown is kept strictly distinct from weak — scoring a missing figure as a failure would penalise a company for our gap.

The bridge is a bucket of 11. Companies below their own industry's ASEAN peer average, with positive evidence momentum, that also pass the profitability and traction screen. Frozen with a run id and a date, so the number is reproducible rather than quoted.

What to look out for, per company. Each verdict carries ESG pros and cons, financial pros and cons, and the specific warnings that apply to it — proximity to a quadrant boundary, provisional metadata, evidence older than the chosen horizon, a high share of self-published sources. The cons are never dimmed or collapsed. A case that lists only reasons to agree is marketing.

And what would change it. Re-score with each signal removed in turn: the tool names the removals that flip the verdict and the distance to every boundary. Backwards, five validation cases replay real runs at their own historical cutoffs — our verdict moving on real dates against a rating that sat still. The case we get wrong is kept in, flagged. A backtest you can only pass is not a backtest.

6 · Verification and the ledger — three layers, named honestly

LayerWhat it is here
1 · SensorsFour-angle live retrieval, the incumbent rating fetched live per company, an industry GHG-intensity benchmark that re-downloads from a free public API in seconds, and a verified basket. Our sensors are documentary, not telemetry — we read filings, regulator actions and exchange notices; we do not meter a smokestack.
2 · VerificationFive guards on gathered evidence: a source URL not in the retrieved snippets is dropped; an undated claim is dropped; a date later than the basket's own cutoff is dropped; source type is decided by domain, never self-declared; and the verified basket is never overwritten. Company-published material is capped at 0.50 confidence and forward-looking language has its weight halved. All of it off-chain, in rules a reviewer can read.
3 · LedgerOne hash per run — id, root, timestamp, 72 bytes — appended to a public test chain. Not a database: nothing can be read back out, and a run id can be written once, ever. That single rule is the whole tamper-evidence story.
The correction we make deliberately: a blockchain is a closed system and cannot reach real-world information, so it can never verify an ESG claim — immutability applied to a false claim only makes it permanent. The chain here is the ledger layer. Verification is done off-chain by readable rules, and the chain records a commitment to what those rules saw. No token, no on-chain scoring, and no licensed data ever written.

Why it is that cheap, and what it costs to scale. The scoring path costs zero tokens — rules route the evidence and deterministic code scores it, with no model reachable from either. All AI cost sits in gathering, charged per sweep of a company rather than per signal: S$39.65/year at 500 companies, S$158.60 at 2,000. The remaining cost is people-time verifying company metadata, priced at day rates taken from live Singapore job postings. Cost per company on this basis is flat, not falling — it only falls once a fixed data licence is spread across clients, and no quote exists yet, so we do not draw that curve. Drawing it before the number arrives is drawing the conclusion first.

8 · Limits we put on the record

  • Zero companies currently clear our "hidden winner" bar, and we did not lower it. Twelve clear the disagreement threshold; every one fails on confidence, because ~92% of what live search returns is company-published and our own rule caps that at 0.50. The honest route is better sources — regulator actions, exchange filings, index decisions — not a smaller number. Lowering our own bar until the screen returns the answer we want is the exact failure this tool exists to catch in others.
  • The foundation basket has an inconvenient second half, and we show it. It returned 55.1% against the index's 6.4% over the study window — and lagged that index by 4.6% year-to-date on a ~45% banking weight, with ESG improvement alone a weak predictor of single-stock performance. Both numbers appear together, always.
  • Some metadata is provisional until reviewed, and says so on screen, with a maker-checker chip and a named reviewer. Verified by us is not an endorsement anyone else gave.
  • Three major raters are absent by law, not by oversight. Their scores are licensed products that cannot be fetched, displayed or redistributed without a contract. We use the one major rater with a free, keyless, per-company endpoint, attributed and on demand.
In one line: a rating tells you a level and asks to be believed; this tells you where it disagrees, on which dated evidence, what the company's own numbers say alongside it, what would change the answer — and leaves a hash behind so you can check, years later, that we are not telling you a different story than we told at the time.
